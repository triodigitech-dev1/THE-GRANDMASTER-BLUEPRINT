# The Regulatory Track: The Roles, Certifications, and Paths of Arbiters and Tournament Organizers

Every chess game that is played under formal conditions—whether a local club tournament or a World Championship match—depends on two unseen pillars: the **arbiter** who enforces the rules and ensures fair play, and the **organizer** who creates the conditions for the event to exist at all. Together, they form the regulatory track of chess: a professional pathway that is distinct from playing and coaching, yet equally essential to the game's survival.

The great arbiter Geurt Gijssen, who served as chief arbiter for the 1987 Kasparov–Karpov World Championship match and chaired the FIDE Rules Commission for 20 years, explained the arbiter's role with characteristic clarity: "The arbiter is not there to win the game for one player or the other. The arbiter is there to ensure that the game is played according to the rules". Takis Nikolopoulos, who chaired the FIDE Arbiters' Commission for two decades and served as Chief Arbiter in three World Chess Olympiads, described his greatest strength as "a keen eye for detail and a comprehensive knowledge of the rules and regulations that govern the game".

This section maps the regulatory track in full: the FIDE arbiter certification ladder, the seminar and norm system, the organizer title pathway, and the profiles of the figures who have shaped chess governance at the highest level.

## Part I: The Arbiter's Path—From National Arbiter to International Arbiter

![The arbiter's role: enforcing the rules and ensuring fair play](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/Garry_Kasparov_2007.jpg/600px-Garry_Kasparov_2007.jpg)

The FIDE arbiter system is structured as a ladder, with clear requirements at each level. FIDE recognizes two international titles: **FIDE Arbiter (FA)** , the entry level, and **International Arbiter (IA)** , the advanced level. The title of International Arbiter can be awarded only to applicants who have already been awarded the title of FIDE Arbiter. Both titles are valid for life.

### National Arbiter (NA)

Before international certification, most arbiters begin at the national level. The title of **Arbiter of National Level (NA)** is under the responsibility of the national federations. FIDE's Arbiters' Commission registers and issues licenses for NAs after recommendation from their federations.

**Profile: Vasant BH**

International Arbiter **Vasant BH** of India began his arbitering journey "accidentally" when his son started playing tournament chess. In the mid-2000s, Indian chess was plagued by organizers who assigned local ratings and manipulated pairings so their own students would win. Vasant confronted one such organizer, who challenged him: "When you become an arbiter yourself, you'll understand the problem, talking from outside is easy". The organizer gave him the Under-7 section to run as a test. Vasant rose to the challenge, armed with chocolates to win over the children. "That's how the journey started," he recalls. Two decades later, he has officiated at the World Cup in Goa and is a familiar face at major Indian tournaments.

### FIDE Arbiter (FA) — The Entry-Level International Title

To become a **FIDE Arbiter**, a candidate must meet several requirements:

1. **Age**: At least **19 years old**.
2. **Knowledge**: "Thorough knowledge of the Laws of Chess and fair play regulations".
3. **Seminar**: Attendance of a **FIDE Arbiters' Seminar** and passing the examination with at least **80% of total points**. The seminar norm is valid for **four years**.
4. **Norms**: Applications for the FA title must include **4 norms** from FIDE-rated events. A norm is a certificate evaluating the arbiter's performance, issued by the Chief Arbiter immediately after the tournament.
5. **Experience**: Prior to the 2026 regulations, candidates needed to hold the NA title for at least one year or have officiated at an additional tournament. The new rules allow candidates to become an FA by holding the NA title for one year **or** officiating at one additional tournament not necessarily valid for an FA norm.

The events used for norms must include at least **two different systems** (e.g., Swiss, Round Robin, Team) and must have participants from at least **three federations** for international events.

### International Arbiter (IA) — The Advanced Title

The **International Arbiter** title is the pinnacle of the arbiter's profession. Requirements include:

1. **Age**: At least **21 years old**.
2. **Prior Title**: Must already hold the FA title.
3. **Experience**: Either hold the FA title for **two years** or have officiated at **three additional tournaments** not necessarily valid for IA norms.
4. **Norms**: Applications for the IA title must include **5 norms** from FIDE-rated events.
5. **Certification Seminar**: From January 2024, candidates for the IA title must attend an **International Arbiter Certification Seminar**. These seminars are a key part of arbiter education, and all FIDE arbiters can participate to get the required norm to become International Arbiters.

The seminar norm is valid for **four years**, while tournament norms are valid for **six years**.

### Arbiter Categories

Beyond the titles, FIDE classifies arbiters into **categories** based on expertise and activity:

- **A' Category** (only IA)
- **B' Category** (only IA)
- **C' Category** (IA and FA)
- **D' Category** (IA and FA)

Arbiters with no activity are classified as **Inactive Arbiters**. These categories determine which events an arbiter can officiate at, with A' Category arbiters eligible for the most prestigious tournaments.

## Part II: The Organizer's Path—From Associate to Premier

![The organizer's role: creating the conditions for chess to happen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/Chess_board_%28german%29.svg/800px-Chess_board_%28german%29.svg.png)

While the arbiter enforces the rules, the **organizer** creates the event. FIDE's regulations define the organizer's role in expansive terms: "Organizer plays a decisive role in planning and conducting official chess tournaments". The organizer's responsibilities include:

- Presenting high-quality bidding documents to FIDE and other chess federations
- Ensuring planning of official chess events in compliance with standards
- Communicating properly with players, arbiters, sponsors, media, and all parties involved
- Forming a strong team to ensure a successful tournament
- Creating a comfortable environment for players and spectators
- Providing conditions for mass media and facilitating chess promotion
- Ensuring attractive environment for sponsors
- Maintaining proper coordination with FIDE bodies and governmental organizations

### FIDE Organizer Titles

FIDE has established a three-tier system of organizer titles:

#### 1. FIDE Associate Organizer

The **Associate Organizer** title is the entry-level tier. It requires a minimum of **three years' experience** organizing at least **five rated competitions** with standard time control. To apply, the National Federation sends the applicant's resume to the FIDE Events Commission (EVE). EVE provides access to online training resources and tests.

#### 2. FIDE International Organizer

The **International Organizer** title is the mid-tier. Requirements include:

- **Seminar**: Attendance and passing an examination in a **Seminar for Organizers**. The seminar lasts **3 days** with a minimum of 15 hours of instruction plus 3 hours for examination.
- **Experience**: Proof of organizing **3 titled tournaments** with participants from at least **3 federations**, including at least one Swiss and one round-robin tournament with standard time control.
- **Languages**: At least **2 languages**.
- **Title Fee**: 100 euros one-time; **Licence Fee**: 100 euros, valid for 4 years.

The seminar covers all regulations of the Events Commission, rating and title regulations, and event management.

#### 3. FIDE Premier Organizer

The **Premier Organizer** title is the highest tier. While the search results do not provide the full requirements, the FIDE Events Commission regulations indicate that the highest tier requires at least **four years' experience as a FIDE International Organizer** and the organization of a **FIDE World Championship event**.

## Part III: Profiles of Legendary Arbiters

### Geurt Gijssen: The Legendary Lawgiver

**Geurt Gijssen**, born in 1934 in Germany and raised in the Netherlands, is one of the most famous chess arbiters in history. A mathematics teacher by profession, he became an International Arbiter in **1979** and went on to serve as Chief Arbiter at the World Championship matches between **Kasparov and Karpov in 1987 and 1990**, as well as Karpov–Kamsky (1996), Karpov–Anand (1998), and Kramnik–Topalov (2006). He was Head Arbiter at four Chess Olympiads (1998, 2000, 2002, 2006) and chaired the FIDE Rules Commission for **20 years**. In 2013, he was awarded the title of **FIDE Honorary Member** in recognition of his outstanding contributions to chess.

Gijssen's legacy is not just his rulings but his writing. He authored several books on chess and was known for his deep understanding of the laws of the game. His advice to young arbiters remains essential: the arbiter must know the rules thoroughly, communicate clearly, and remain calm under pressure.

### Takis Nikolopoulos: The Diplomat of the Board

**Panagiotis "Takis" Nikolopoulos**, born in 1957 in Athens, Greece, is a civil engineer by profession who dedicated his life to chess arbitration. He became an **International Arbiter in 1993** and served as **Chief Arbiter in three World Chess Olympiads and six World Championship matches** for both men and women. From **1998 to 2018**, he chaired the **FIDE Arbiters' Commission**, continuing as Honorary Chairman until 2022. He is also a **FIDE Lecturer**, conducting arbiters' seminars worldwide, and serves as Tournament Director and Rating Officer of the Greek Chess Federation.

Nikolopoulos's philosophy of arbitration emphasizes communication and diplomacy: "Effective communication is absolutely necessary in the role of an Arbiter. My experience has taught me to communicate clearly and diplomatically with players, organizers, and colleagues-arbiters".

### Mahdi Abdulrahim: The Youngest International Arbiter

**Mahdi Abdulrahim** of the UAE began his arbitration journey at age 16, learning from experienced arbiters at the Dubai Chess and Culture Club. His turning point came in **1986** when he served as an arbiter at the **27th Chess Olympiad in Dubai**, where he met world champions Anatoly Karpov and Garry Kasparov. "Meeting these legends was a dream come true," he recalls. At **24 years old**, he was awarded the **International Arbiter title during the FIDE Olympiad Congress**, making him the **youngest International Arbiter at that time**.

Abdulrahim went on to serve as **Chairman of the FIDE Rules Commission** from 2018 to 2022 and has been a councillor for the FIDE Arbiters' Commission for many years. He has conducted seminars in Arabic and English in **18 Arab countries** and some Asian and African countries.

### Lothar Schmid: The Man Who Saved Fischer–Spassky

**Lothar Schmid**, a German Grandmaster who represented West Germany at eleven Chess Olympiads, is most famous as the **chief arbiter of the 1972 Fischer–Spassky match**. His handling of that historic encounter—navigating the political tensions, the players' demands, and the media frenzy—is a masterclass in arbitering under pressure.

## Part IV: The Synergy of Arbiter and Organizer

While the arbiter and organizer have distinct roles, they are deeply interdependent. The FIDE regulations for organizers emphasize that the organizer must "ensure the best possible conditions" for the arbiter to do their job, while the arbiter must "communicate properly with players, arbiters, sponsors, media and all other parties involved in the event".

**James Eade**, an American chess master and organizer, exemplifies this synergy. His book _No Blame, No Shame_ recounts "the struggles of addiction, the power of community, and the transformative impact of chess on his life". Eade founded the Eade Foundation, which uses chess to empower underserved communities. His message is one of radical hope: "no matter the circumstances, everyone has the potential to recover and transform their lives".

The **organizer's role** is also increasingly recognized as a professional pathway in its own right. As FIDE's regulations note, "Organizer plays a decisive role in planning and conducting official chess tournaments" and "serves as the guarantee for successful chess tournament, thus, indirectly gaining trust and credibility and ensures an increase in the number of active chess players".

## Conclusion: The Unsung Architects of Chess

The regulatory track of chess—arbiters and organizers—is not merely a support structure. It is the foundation upon which every game, every tournament, and every championship rests. Without arbiters, there is no fair play; without organizers, there is no place to play.

The pathway to becoming an elite arbiter or organizer is demanding but clearly defined. For arbiters, it begins with national certification, progresses through the **FIDE Arbiter** and **International Arbiter** titles, and requires a combination of seminar attendance, tournament norms, and years of experience. For organizers, it begins with the **Associate Organizer** title, advances to **International Organizer**, and culminates in the **Premier Organizer** tier.

The figures profiled here—Geurt Gijssen, Takis Nikolopoulos, Mahdi Abdulrahim, Lothar Schmid, Vasant BH—represent the highest ideals of the regulatory track. They are not merely rule-keepers; they are diplomats, educators, and guardians of the game's integrity.

As Takis Nikolopoulos put it: "My commitment to upholding the spirit of fair play and ensuring an appropriate playing environment has been a guiding principle throughout my tenure". That commitment is the essence of the regulatory track. The board is set. The players are ready. The arbiter is watching. The organizer has ensured that everything is in place.

**The game can begin.**
