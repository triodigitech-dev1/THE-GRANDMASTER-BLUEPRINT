# The Anatomy of a Comeback: Case Studies in Resilience and Reversing Lost Positions

**Trigger Warning: This article discusses suicide, addiction, and mental health crises. If you are in distress, please reach out to a local crisis line.**

A chess game, like a life, can appear lost long before it is over. A piece down, a position in ruins, a clock ticking toward zero—the external signs all point to defeat. Yet chess history is filled with players who refused to accept the verdict of the position, who found resources where none seemed to exist, and who turned certain loss into triumph. This document explores the anatomy of those comebacks, both on the board and beyond it, examining the psychological mechanisms that make resilience possible and the stories of those for whom chess became a lifeline when everything else had failed.

## Part I: The Anatomy of a Comeback on the Board

### The Psychology of the Lost Position

What happens in the mind of a player who realizes they are losing? Research on chess psychology describes a cascade of cognitive and emotional responses: self-pity, despair, a collapse of confidence, and the temptation to resign prematurely. As one analysis of comeback psychology notes, "the key lessons are to avoid self-pity, keep pieces on the board, create complications, pose constant problems, and play actively as if you're not worse—because pressure often causes the opponent to falter".

The mechanism is counterintuitive but well-documented. When a player is winning, their decision-making often deteriorates. They become cautious, defensive, and fearful of losing their advantage. The player who is losing has nothing to lose—and this psychological freedom can be a powerful weapon. The comeback artist understands that a lost position is not a lost game; it is simply a different kind of challenge.

### Case Study 1: Kasparov's Final Game, Seville 1987

The 1987 World Championship match between Garry Kasparov and Anatoly Karpov is often cited as the greatest comeback in chess history. Before the final game, Karpov led 12–11 and needed only a draw to reclaim the title. Kasparov, the reigning champion, needed a win with the white pieces—a feat that had not been accomplished in a must-win final game of a World Championship since Lasker vs. Schlechter in 1910.

The pressure was immense. Kasparov later described the moment: "The very next day after this catastrophe, I had to take the white pieces into a must-win game 24". He chose the English Opening—a quiet, positional choice that surprised Karpov, who was expecting a sharp attacking attempt. Kasparov played in Karpov's own style, slowly accumulating small advantages. Karpov, needing only a draw, played passively, and his position gradually deteriorated. On move 64, Karpov resigned. Kasparov had retained his title.

![Garry Kasparov and Anatoly Karpov during their 1987 World Championship match in Seville](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/Garry_Kasparov_2007.jpg/600px-Garry_Kasparov_2007.jpg)

The comeback was not merely tactical. It was psychological. Kasparov understood that Karpov, playing for a draw, would be vulnerable to pressure. By refusing to accept the inevitability of defeat, he created the conditions for victory.

### Case Study 2: Fischer's Forfeit and Recovery, Reykjavik 1972

Bobby Fischer's 1972 World Championship match against Boris Spassky began in disaster. Fischer forfeited Game 2 by failing to appear, putting him down 0–2 in a match where the first player to 12.5 points would win. The chess world was stunned. Fischer's behavior seemed to confirm every fear about his psychological stability.

Then something remarkable happened. Fischer won Game 3, drew Game 4, and won Game 5, levelling the match at 2½–2½. He went on to win the match 12.5–8.5, ending a 35-year Soviet stranglehold on the World Championship. The forfeit that seemed to signal his collapse became the prelude to his greatest triumph.

![Bobby Fischer during the 1972 World Championship match](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/Bobby_Fischer_1962.jpg/600px-Bobby_Fischer_1962.jpg)

Fischer's comeback was rooted in a single-minded refusal to accept the narrative of defeat. He did not panic; he did not change his approach. He simply played his game, and his game was better than anyone else's.

### Case Study 3: Korchnoi's Rally, Baguio City 1978

Viktor Korchnoi's 1978 World Championship match against Anatoly Karpov was as much a political and psychological battle as a chess contest. Korchnoi, a Soviet defector, was challenging the Soviet system's champion. The match was played in Baguio City, Philippines, under bizarre conditions: Korchnoi wore mirrored glasses to ward off alleged hypnosis, and the two camps engaged in a war of psychological manipulation.

On the board, Karpov surged to a 5–2 lead. Korchnoi appeared finished. Then he won three of the next four games, levelling the score at 5–5 and forcing a decisive final game. Karpov ultimately won that game and retained his title, but Korchnoi's rally from 5–2 down remains one of the most remarkable comebacks in World Championship history.

![Viktor Korchnoi, the Soviet defector who nearly toppled Karpov](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6e/Viktor_Korchnoi.jpg/600px-Viktor_Korchnoi.jpg)

Korchnoi's resilience was forged in adversity. He had defected from the Soviet Union, leaving behind his family and his support system. He had nothing to lose—and that freedom made him dangerous.

### Case Study 4: Carlsen's Fightback, New York 2016

Magnus Carlsen's 2016 World Championship match against Sergey Karjakin was perhaps the most psychologically demanding of his career. For the first time, Carlsen found himself trailing in a World Championship match. After losing Game 8, he described his state of mind with brutal honesty: he believed defeat was "certain".

What followed was a masterclass in resilience. Carlsen did not collapse; he regrouped. He won Game 10 to level the match, then dominated the rapid tiebreaks to retain his title. The Guardian described it as "the greatest champions find the path to victory when they're not at their best".

![Magnus Carlsen during the 2016 World Championship match](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5e/Magnus_Carlsen_2014.jpg/600px-Magnus_Carlsen_2014.jpg)

Carlsen's comeback was not about playing better chess—he had been playing well throughout. It was about managing his own mind. He later spoke openly about his emotions: "My emotions are usually outside my body and that's not what you usually connect to a chess player". His willingness to acknowledge and work through his emotional state, rather than suppressing it, was central to his recovery.

## Part II: Profiles in Resilience

### Samuel Reshevsky (1911–1992): The Comeback After the Loss

Samuel Reshevsky, the American grandmaster and eight-time U.S. Champion, was a prodigy who learned chess at four and was touring Europe at eight. In the 1973 U.S. Championship playoff, Reshevsky suffered a devastating third-round loss to Robert Byrne. In a short match-tournament where each player played only two games against each opponent, the pressure to recover immediately was immense. As one contemporary account noted, "it is impossible to keep cool after a loss. If the point is to be made up at all, it must be done at once".

Reshevsky's response was characteristic. In the fourth round, he defeated Lubomir Kavalek in a finely contested game, "smashing back into contention". His comeback was not flamboyant; it was methodical, disciplined, and relentless.

### Alexei Shirov (born 1972): From Last Place to Tournament Victory

Alexei Shirov, the Latvian-Spanish grandmaster, is one of the most brilliant and volatile attackers in modern chess. In 2009, he won the prestigious M-Tel Masters in Bulgaria, one of the strongest tournaments of the year. Less than two weeks later, he played in the Karpov Tournament in Poikovsky, Russia—and suffered a catastrophic collapse. He lost his first four games, drew the next two, then lost again. His final score was four draws, five losses, and no wins: last place.

The chess world wrote him off. But Shirov has always been "mercurial"—"prone to blind spots, and has had runs of poor play throughout his career. But he always bounces back". Just days later, at the Kings Tournament in Bazna, Romania, he started with a victory over Gata Kamsky. The comeback had begun.

![Alexei Shirov, who recovered from a last-place finish to win again](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/Alexei_Shirov.jpg/600px-Alexei_Shirov.jpg)

Shirov's resilience is a lesson in the power of starting over. A terrible tournament does not define a career. The next game is always a new beginning.

### Koneru Humpy (born 1987): "I Will Keep Fighting"

In 2024, Koneru Humpy, the Indian grandmaster and former World Rapid Champion, thought about leaving chess. She was dealing with the loss of her grandparent and a slip in her ranking points. For a player of her caliber, the thought of walking away was a sign of how deeply she had been affected. Yet by 2025, she was fighting in a World Cup final. Her own words captured the essence of her comeback: "I will keep fighting".

Humpy's story is a reminder that resilience is not the absence of doubt. It is the decision to continue despite the doubt.

### D. Gukesh (born 2006): The Djokovician Trait

D. Gukesh, the teenage World Champion, has demonstrated a remarkable ability to "survive during position of disadvantage against his rivals and come out on the winning side". Analysts have described his resilience as "Djokovician"—a reference to the tennis champion's ability to win when not playing his best. In a 2025 Norway Chess game against Arjun Erigaisi, Gukesh "gradually clawed his way back into the game with precise calculations and a calm defence. In a remarkable turnaround, he neutralised Erigaisi's advantage and seized control".

Gukesh's youth is deceptive. His psychological maturity—his refusal to panic, his willingness to defend for hours, his belief that the game is never over—is the mark of a player who has internalized the deepest lesson of chess: the position is not lost until you resign.

## Part III: Chess as a Lifeline—Comebacks in Life

### John Healy: The Grass Arena

John Healy's story is perhaps the most powerful example of chess as a tool of redemption. Born in 1943, Healy was a promising boxer whose career collapsed under alcoholism. He descended into homelessness and violence, living on the streets of London as a "street drinker". He was imprisoned multiple times, and his life seemed irreparably broken.

In prison, a cellmate taught him chess. Healy found in the game "an alternative addiction—chess took over my mind". He gave up alcohol and began playing obsessively, eventually becoming a tournament champion. His autobiography, _The Grass Arena_, won Britain's highest honor for autobiography in 1988 and was later made into a film.

![John Healy, whose life was transformed by chess in prison](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8a/John_Healy_author.jpg/600px-John_Healy_author.jpg)

Healy's story is not a fairy tale. He later acknowledged that he had simply "given up one addiction for another". But the replacement addiction—chess—gave him structure, purpose, and a community. It gave him a reason to live. As he wrote in _The Grass Arena_, chess was "a new arena where the conflicts are subject to unfamiliar rules"—a world in which he could rebuild himself.

### James Eade: From Addiction to Advocacy

James Eade, an American chess master and organizer, has shared his deeply personal journey from addiction to recovery in his book _No Blame, No Shame_. Eade recounts "the struggles of addiction, the power of community, and the transformative impact of chess on his life". The book is described as "a heartfelt narrative of hope, resilience, and redemption", in which "chess provided structure, purpose, and strength during his darkest moments".

Eade's story is not merely about personal recovery. It is about what recovery makes possible. He founded the Eade Foundation, which uses chess to empower underserved communities across the globe. His message is one of radical hope: "no matter the circumstances, everyone has the potential to recover and transform their lives".

### An Anonymous Prisoner: "Chess Probably Kept Me From Suicide"

In a report on chess in prisons by Carl Portman, an anonymous prisoner identified only as "J (HMP Littlehey)" offered a testimony that is as stark as it is powerful: **"With brutal honesty, chess probably kept me from suicide."** Another prisoner, identified as "G (HMP Stafford)", said: "Playing chess will give you a reason to come out of your cell and socialize with your fellow players, plus it is good for your mental health as it makes you concentrate".

The report documents numerous such testimonies. One prisoner with Asperger's Syndrome described the experience of chess "clicking into place": "it is like the board lights up. It is an amazing sensation". Another simply said: **"Chess keeps me sane in an insane place."**.

These are not exaggerations. The structure of chess—its clear rules, its logical progression, its requirement for focused attention—provides exactly what people in crisis often lack: a frame for their day, a reason to engage with others, and a tangible sense of progress.

### The Anonymous Lichess Player: "Something to Live For"

On the Lichess forum, a user described a period of profound despair: "I remember that about 4 years ago my life was worth absolutely nothing, and all I did all day was play chess. It was silly and I was just wasting my time, but it was something to live for".

This testimony captures something essential about chess's therapeutic value. It is not a cure for depression. It does not erase trauma or poverty. But it provides something that can be enough: a reason to show up the next day. As the author of the "Finding Inner Piece" blog writes, "Not a cure. Just a reason to show up the next day. Sometimes that is enough".

### David Navara: Depression and the Threat of Suicide

David Navara, the Czech grandmaster and one of the world's top players, has spoken openly about his experience of depression and suicidal thoughts. In 2025, he revealed that he had been "on the verge of suicide" after being accused of cheating by former World Champion Vladimir Kramnik. Navara described "crying for hours" and feeling that his "desperate" pleas for help from FIDE were ignored.

Navara's story is a reminder that even the most accomplished chess players are not immune to mental health crises. The pressure of elite competition, the isolation of professional chess, and the psychological toll of public accusations can be overwhelming. His willingness to speak openly about his struggle is an act of courage that may help others in similar positions.

## Conclusion: The Anatomy of Resilience

What makes a comeback possible? The case studies in this document suggest several common elements.

**First, the refusal to accept defeat prematurely.** Kasparov, Fischer, Korchnoi, and Carlsen all faced positions—on and off the board—that appeared hopeless. They did not resign. They continued to fight, and their persistence created opportunities that would not have existed otherwise.

**Second, the ability to manage emotions.** Resilience is not the absence of fear, doubt, or despair. It is the capacity to act despite these emotions. Carlsen's admission that he believed defeat was "certain" did not prevent him from winning. It was his honesty about his emotional state, combined with his refusal to be defined by it, that made his comeback possible.

**Third, the presence of structure.** For people in crisis—whether in prison, in addiction, or in depression—chess provides something essential: a structured activity with clear rules, measurable progress, and a community of others who share the same language. As the "Finding Inner Piece" blog notes, structured activities "interrupt this [collapse of structure]—not by fixing the problem, but by giving you a frame. A reason to sit down. A set of rules existing regardless of how you woke up this morning".

**Fourth, the power of a fresh start.** Every chess game begins from the same position. No matter how badly the last game went, the next one offers a clean slate. This is a profound psychological gift. For someone whose life feels irreparably broken, the chessboard offers a world in which starting over is not just possible—it is the norm.

The anatomy of a comeback, then, is not a mystery. It is a combination of persistence, emotional regulation, structure, and hope. Chess cultivates all of these qualities. It teaches players that a lost position is not a lost game. And for some, it teaches something even more important: that a lost life is not a lost life. There is always another move.
